2. **Project name.** Hearsay.
3. **Short project description.** Agents in a grid world find things, can share them only at home, and die. Does knowledge outlive the one who found it? A colony of language-model bees in a maze must find seasonal bloom while hornets that evolve by natural selection hunt them. The bees' prompt never names bees, flowers, hornets or seasons; it says, among the rules of moving and sensing, that their life falls and that something is worth finding. They keep their own memories and can talk only inside the hive. We test, against pre-registered controls, whether the colony learns, whether knowledge outlives the bees that found it, whether the hornets adapt to the colony, and whether two meaningless sounds come to mean something. Every pre-registered hypothesis with results so far (round 1 and the E5 follow-up) failed its controls, and the write-up reports what the controls showed instead: the bees' caution is not learned from experience (it comes from the model's prior, the prompt's wording, or both), and sound meaning comes from labels. In one follow-up seed, talk at home carried a place to a newborn that then found it. A second round with abstract wording (pre-registered, 3 to 4 simulated years per run) found no discovery of the reward rule and no benefit from talk (H10, H7 and H8 unsupported). On a single seed per arm, moving away at first sight of a hornet fell from 0.893 with the round 1 words to a mean of 0.147 with abstract words: evidence that the caution came from the wording.
