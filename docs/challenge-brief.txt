﻿DIGITAL LIFE
Hackathon — Master Brief & Repository Specification
Theme: Make Something That Lives.
Format: Open-ended technical hackathon
Submission: Public GitHub repository
Core requirement: Build an artificial system that exhibits some meaningful property of life, adaptation, evolution, autonomy or emergent behaviour.
________________


1. The challenge
We want you to build something that lives.
Not necessarily biological life. Not necessarily an AI agent. Not necessarily something useful.
We are interested in artificial systems that exhibit interesting behaviours: things that evolve, adapt, learn, interact, reproduce, compete, cooperate, develop memory, construct environments, or demonstrate emergent complexity.
You might build:
* An artificial organism that evolves through generations.
* A digital ecosystem with competing species and resource constraints.
* A society of autonomous agents that develops its own culture.
* A self-modifying program that evolves its own architecture.
* A simulated world where intelligence emerges from simple rules.
* A persistent artificial character that develops a personality through experience.
* A synthetic biological system.
* A multi-agent environment with unexpected emergent behaviour.
* An evolutionary algorithm that discovers solutions nobody explicitly programmed.
* Something entirely different.
You can use machine learning, reinforcement learning, evolutionary computation, LLMs, neural networks, cellular automata, robotics, distributed systems, physics simulations, or none of these.
There is no prescribed technology stack.
There is no requirement to build a commercial product.
There is no requirement to use AI.
The objective is to explore the possibilities of digital life.
2. What we care about
We are particularly interested in five properties.
A. Autonomy
Does the system do things independently, rather than simply responding to a sequence of predefined instructions?
B. Persistence
Does it retain meaningful state, memory, identity or environmental information over time?
C. Adaptation
Can it change its behaviour in response to experience, feedback or changing conditions?
D. Interaction
Can it interact with other entities, its environment or other systems in interesting ways?
E. Emergence
Can complex or unexpected behaviour arise from relatively simple underlying mechanisms?
These are areas of exploration, not mandatory checkboxes. A project does not need to demonstrate all five.
We would rather see one genuinely interesting property explored deeply than five superficial features.
3. Technical expectations
We want to see actual engineering and experimentation.
A beautiful interface is welcome, but a polished frontend cannot substitute for a technically interesting system.
Depending on your project, relevant technical work might include:
* Novel system architectures.
* Agent orchestration and coordination.
* Memory architectures and persistence.
* Evolutionary selection and mutation.
* Reinforcement learning.
* Multi-agent communication protocols.
* Environment modelling and simulation.
* Self-modifying or self-replicating code (within controlled environments).
* Novel training or inference approaches.
* Distributed computation.
* Behavioural evaluation.
* Quantitative analysis of emergent properties.
You are encouraged to experiment, take risks and build things that might fail.
A negative result with good experimental methodology is more interesting than an unsupported claim of emergent intelligence.
4. Use of AI during development
AI tools are permitted and encouraged.
You may use coding agents, LLMs, autonomous development systems, AI-assisted research, code generation, debugging tools, model APIs and other AI-enabled development workflows.
We are interested in understanding how people build with AI, not pretending that AI wasn’t involved.
However, AI-generated code is not evidence of technical contribution by itself.
Every submission must include a transparent account of AI usage.
4.1 AI disclosure
Include an AI_DISCLOSURE.md file in your repository.
Document:
1. Which AI systems and models were used.
2. Which coding agents or autonomous tools were used.
3. What each tool was used for.
4. Which components were primarily AI-generated.
5. Which components were primarily written or designed by the participants.
6. How generated code was reviewed, tested and modified.
7. Whether AI systems were used to design the architecture or research the underlying concepts.
8. Any significant limitations, mistakes or unexpected behaviours introduced by AI assistance.
Where possible, provide approximate usage information, including model versions and dates.
Do not fabricate exact token counts or usage statistics if they are unavailable.
4.2 AI development philosophy
We do not penalise extensive AI usage.
We do care about whether participants understand what they have built.
You should be able to explain:
* How your system works.
* Why you chose its architecture.
* What its key mechanisms are.
* Where AI contributed.
* What you personally investigated or implemented.
* What failed and why.
The distinction is between using AI as an engineering tool and submitting an artefact that nobody on the team can explain.
5. Agent traces and development provenance
This is a mandatory part of the submission.
If you use AI agents during development, we want to see evidence of their activity.
We are interested in the development process itself: how agents were instructed, what tasks they performed, what tools they used, what they produced, and how humans interacted with and supervised them.
Create an agent_traces/ directory.
Where available, include:
* Agent session logs.
* Task descriptions and prompts.
* Tool invocation records.
* Tool results.
* Code-generation activity.
* Relevant model and version information.
* Agent-to-agent communication.
* Human interventions and approvals.
* Significant errors and recovery attempts.
* Links between agent tasks and resulting code changes.
Suggested trace format
Use JSONL wherever practical, with one event per line.
Example:
{
  "timestamp": "2026-11-06T10:30:00Z",
  "session_id": "development-001",
  "agent_id": "architecture-agent",
  "model": "example-model",
  "event_type": "tool_call",
  "task": "Design organism state transition system",
  "tool": "python",
  "input_summary": "Test alternative mutation mechanisms",
  "output_summary": "Identified three viable approaches",
  "human_intervention": false
}
This is an illustrative schema, not a requirement to use a particular agent framework.
Trace integrity
* Preserve original machine-generated traces where possible.
* Do not invent or reconstruct traces and present them as original.
* Clearly identify manually summarised or redacted material.
* Include timestamps and session identifiers where available.
* Link significant agent activity to relevant commits or artefacts where possible.
* Document missing trace data rather than silently omitting it.
Important: We want execution and development traces, not private hidden chain-of-thought. Do not submit confidential internal reasoning, API credentials, personal information, private conversations or sensitive data. Summaries of reasoning and documented engineering decisions are sufficient.
If you did not use AI agents, state this explicitly in your disclosure. Do not manufacture agent traces.
6. Experiments and evidence
Create an EXPERIMENTS.md file.
Document the experiments that informed your project.
For each significant experiment, include:
* Hypothesis.
* Experimental setup.
* Variables or parameters.
* Observations.
* Results.
* What you learned.
* What you would investigate next.
Where applicable, include quantitative measurements.
Examples:
* How does population diversity change over time?
* Does an organism develop different behaviour after repeated environmental exposure?
* Can agents independently develop a communication protocol?
* Does a system maintain identity after prolonged operation?
* Does the ecosystem collapse under resource scarcity?
* Does mutation improve or degrade performance?
* Can an emergent behaviour be reproduced across independent runs?
Include negative results.
We are not expecting every experiment to succeed.
We are interested in whether you can distinguish genuine emergent behaviour from hardcoded behaviour, randomness, prompt effects or artefacts of your experimental setup.
7. Reproducibility
A judge should be able to understand and, ideally, run your project.
Include:
* Installation instructions.
* Dependencies and versions.
* Environment requirements.
* Configuration instructions.
* Model/API requirements, if applicable.
* Example commands.
* A reproducible demonstration.
* Expected outputs.
* Known limitations.
Where practical, include:
* Fixed random seeds.
* Example datasets.
* Configuration files.
* Model checkpoints or links to accessible checkpoints.
* Test cases.
* Evaluation scripts.
* Docker or equivalent environment configuration.
Do not commit API keys, credentials, secrets or private datasets.
Use .env.example to document required environment variables without including their actual values.

Every team must submit a public GitHub repository.
The repository must be accessible to judges without requiring private organisational access.

GitHub requirements
1. Create a new public repository.
2. Give it a meaningful project name.
3. Include a descriptive README.
4. Include the mandatory AI disclosure.
5. Include the experiments documentation.
6. Include agent traces where applicable.
7. Commit the actual source code.
8. Include a licence appropriate to your intended sharing permissions.
9. Ensure there are no exposed credentials or sensitive information.
10. Verify that the repository can be accessed by someone outside your team.
Your repository should reflect the actual development process, not just a final snapshot.
We recommend meaningful commits throughout development.
Do not squash or rewrite history merely to make the project appear to have been developed differently.
9. README specification
Your root README.md is the first thing judges will see.
It should contain:
Project name
Give your creation an identity.
One-line description
What did you build?
The idea
Explain the concept and what makes it interesting.
Why digital life?
What property of artificial life are you exploring?
Demonstration
Include a video, GIF, screenshots or live demo link.
Technical architecture
Explain the system’s major components and how they interact.
How it works
Describe the underlying mechanisms, algorithms and models.
Getting started
Provide installation and execution instructions.
Experiments and findings
Summarise the most interesting observations.
AI usage
Link to AI_DISCLOSURE.md.
Agent traces
Explain how AI agents were used and link to the relevant trace directory, if applicable.
Team
List the contributors and their roles.
Future directions
What would you investigate if you had another month?
10. Demonstration
Every team must provide a demonstration of its creation.
This can be:
* A live interactive system.
* A recorded demonstration.
* A simulation.
* A visualisation.
* An experiment running in real time.
* A command-line demonstration.
* A physical or robotic prototype.
We want to see the system doing something.
A presentation explaining what it could theoretically do is not a substitute for demonstrating what it actually does.
If the system is computationally expensive or requires specialised infrastructure, provide a recorded demonstration and a reproducible smaller-scale experiment.
11. Judging criteria
We will judge submissions across five dimensions.
Category
	Weight
	Technical depth
	30%
	Originality and creativity
	25%
	Demonstrated behaviour and experimentation
	20%
	Engineering and reproducibility
	15%
	Effective use of tools, including AI
	10%
	Technical depth — 30%
Is there genuine technical substance?
Are the underlying mechanisms interesting, well-designed and appropriately implemented?
Originality — 25%
Is this an unusual or interesting idea?
Does it explore something beyond conventional application development?
Behaviour and experimentation — 20%
Does the system actually demonstrate the properties it claims?
Are there interesting observations, experiments or emergent behaviours?
Engineering — 15%
Is the project understandable, reproducible and well documented?
Tool usage — 10%
Were available tools used effectively?
Is AI usage transparent?
Can the team explain its development process?
We do not award points simply for using the largest model, the most tokens, the most agents or the most elaborate frontend.

2. Project name.

The repository is the primary submission. Judges should be able to understand the project from its documentation and inspect its underlying implementation.

THE POINT
We aren’t looking for another wrapper around an API.
We aren’t looking for another dashboard.
We aren’t looking for another slightly different productivity tool.
We want to see people exploring what happens when computation starts behaving in ways that look increasingly like life.
Something that evolves.
Something that adapts.
Something that surprises its creators.
Something that develops a world of its own.
Make something that lives.
